## Analysis

After this week's reading and my conversations and interviews, I believe that the infrastructure problems in the United States are not merely caused by a single rule. Potter focused on the National Environmental Law and demonstrated how environmental reviews have slowed down due to institutions' concerns about litigation. Doomberg focuses on a larger issue, believing that the legal system will delay projects in many different ways. Howard, on the other hand, pays more attention to excessive red tape and hopes that more responsibility will be shouldered by government officials. Wang compared the United States with a system that places more emphasis on engineering (China) and asked whether the United States spent too much time on the review process rather than actually building things.

These ideas are different, but they are closely related to each other. If government officials are more concerned about being blamed or prosecuted and focus their energy on avoiding risks rather than making decisions, they have reasons to slow down their actions.

I think if we want to improve this, perhaps a major institution can be held responsible for a major project. The institution should still clearly explain its decision to the public. This makes it easier to know who is responsible and reduces the problem of many institutions reviewing the same project again and again, cuts down on these cumbersome and repetitive tasks, and speeds up the process. The advantage of doing this is to make faster and more predictable decisions. This will help projects such as power lines, transportation and clean energy to move forward more quickly.

But everything has two sides. If this process is too fast, the residents around the project may not have enough time and opportunity to speak out, and the real environmental problems may also be overlooked. A project can help many people, but those living near it may have to deal with noise, pollution or changes in the land. So I think the solution is not to abolish environmental reviews. The more difficult goal is to maintain useful reviews while reducing those that will only increase delays.

One thing my conversation partner told me was, "More scrutiny doesn't mean better decisions." She also said that if a project affects their place of residence, people still have the right to speak out because they are the ones who truly bear the consequences of the project directly. This makes me feel that the real problem is not just about speed. This is also trust.

At present, my biggest problem is whether people will trust an institution with more power. A faster system might do better on paper, but if people don't trust the decision-makers or even don't know who they are, they might still oppose the project. For me, this is the most difficult part to solve.
