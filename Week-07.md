# Week 7: The Orchard Stream Buffer Initiative

## Proposition Title

The Orchard Stream Buffer Initiative

## The Ballot Question

Should New York State start a three-year pilot in which up to five apple orchards replace small areas of land near streams with native plants to reduce water pollution? Public grants would cover most installation costs, while farmers would pay the rest and maintain the plants. A YES vote would support this voluntary pilot.

## What's the Deal?

Autumn has arrived. Apple orchards are part of New York State's economy and autumn traditions. They offer fruits, attracting tourists to local farms and also bringing business to nearby businesses. But at the same time, agricultural runoff from orchards may also have a certain impact on the environment. For instance, soil, fertilizers and pesticides may be carried into nearby rivers.

Therefore, we propose such an idea: We will invite up to five apple orchards in New York State to transform the riverfront land with a total length of approximately 5,000 feet into a natural plant buffer zone 35 feet wide, with a total area of approximately four acres.

The upper limit of the project's construction budget is $50,000, of which up to $37,500 comes from public grants and farmers bear at least $12,500. In addition, farmers also need to give up part of the planting area and be responsible for the maintenance of the buffer zone.

In exchange, the river water will become cleaner, and local residents and wildlife may benefit. Meanwhile, the economic operation of the apple orchard can still run normally.

## The Best Case for YES

This plan is not intended to affect the normal operation of the apple orchard. This is very important because apple orchards make a significant contribution to the local area. It can provide job opportunities for the local community, attract customers to boost the tourism economy, and is also part of the autumn tradition for many people.

On the contrary, we merely hope that farmers will set aside a small portion of the land close to the river to protect the local water source. For instance, planting plant buffer belts can slow down agricultural runoff and intercept some sediment and pollutants.

Government grants can cover most of the construction costs, making it easier for farmers who are willing to participate to accept this change. Moreover, the technologies and policies we use are not new. The existing agricultural water quality protection programs of the United States Department of Agriculture and New York State have already supported similar measures.

If the pilot program is successful, the county government may consider inviting more orchards to join in the future. In this way, we will have the opportunity to understand whether sacrificing a small amount of land can really improve the environment without seriously affecting the local apple industry.

## The Best Case for NO

The proposal sounds reasonable, but it may not be a good use of public money.

We cannot promise that a 35-foot buffer will remove enough pollution to justify its cost at every orchard. Soil type, slope, rainfall, and farm practices all matter. The streams may receive pollution from other properties, so improvements might be hard to measure.

Farmers would also lose productive land and pay part of the installation cost, which could hurt smaller farms more. Some may refuse to participate, leaving the pilot with no suitable sites.

A three-year program might be too short to see the full benefits of newly planted trees and shrubs. And public money spent here cannot go toward other needs, such as roads, schools, or different water-quality projects.

## Where Is the Body Buried?
The question we are most concerned about is whether there are really enough farmers willing to participate in this program. After all, even if government grants can cover most of the costs, farmers still need to lose some land that could have been used to grow apples, and at the same time, they have to pay for maintenance. This is a considerable expense for them. For some small apple orchards, it can even be regarded as a considerable burden.
Meanwhile, our budget of $50,000 is only an estimate. The actual cost may be even higher. Another problem is that plant buffer zones cannot prevent all types of pollution. Some agricultural runoff may directly enter rivers through drainage pipes or other means.
If too few farmers participate or the plant buffer zone fails to achieve the expected effect, the actual environmental benefits may be far lower than our original expectations.

## Why Isn't This Already Happening?

If plant buffering can improve the environment, why don't all orchards adopt this method?

The reason is that the costs that farmers need to bear are rather heavy. They not only need to lose some land and remove apple trees, but also maintain the newly planted plants. However, the benefits brought by the improvement of water quality are shared by local residents and the ecological environment. In other words, these benefits are not eye-catching enough. Therefore, it is quite understandable that not many people are particularly willing to do so.

Government grants can alleviate this problem, but the funds need to be applied for through competition, and the entire process is rather time-consuming. Moreover, it requires cooperation between landowners and environmental protection workers.
Some farmers may prefer to keep apple trees that can generate income, especially when the future environmental benefits are still uncertain. While nearby residents hope for improved water quality, they may not be willing to use public tax revenue to support private farms.

The above reasons can explain why such a seemingly beneficial environmental protection plan will not be automatically and widely implemented.
